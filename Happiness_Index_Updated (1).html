Criterion A: Knowing and Understanding

Problem Statement

In 2026, India ranked 116th out of 147 countries on the World Happiness Index with a score of 4.54/10. This is lower than Nepal (99th) and Pakistan (104th), while Finland came 1st with 7.76.

Background Info

The World Happiness Report comes out every year. People are asked to rate their life on a ladder from 0 (worst) to 10 (best). This is called the Cantril ladder.

The score is explained using 6 factors:

  1. GDP per capita
  2. Social support
  3. Healthy life expectancy
  4. Freedom to make choices
  5. Generosity
  6. Perceptions of corruption

India went up from 118th in 2025 to 116th in 2026 but is still near the bottom. Social support is one of India's weakest factors. The 2026 report also says using social media for more than about 1 hour a day is linked to lower happiness.

Criterion C: Communicating

Speech: Why India Needs to Take Happiness Seriously

About 5 minutes Audience: students, parents and teachers Purpose: to make people aware

Good morning everyone.

I want to start with a question. If your life was a ladder from 0 to 10, where 0 is the worst life you can imagine and 10 is the best, which step would you be on?

Hold that number in your head, because this is exactly the question the World Happiness Report asks people in almost 150 countries every year. In 2026, the average answer in Finland was 7.76. The average answer in India was 4.54. That puts India at 116th out of 147 countries, below our neighbours Nepal and Pakistan.

1. Why the happiness indicator is important

Most of the time we measure a country by money: GDP, growth, how many companies it has. India's economy is one of the fastest growing in the world. But money alone does not tell us if people's lives are actually going well.

The happiness index looks at six things together: income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make choices, generosity and corruption. So it shows us the whole picture. A country can get richer and still have people who feel lonely, stressed and unsupported. That is why this indicator matters. It tells governments what GDP misses.

It also matters for us as young people. Unhappy students find it harder to learn, unhappy workers are less productive, and unhappiness is linked to poorer health. Happiness is not a luxury. It affects everything else.

2. How a country can improve happiness

So how do countries like Finland stay at the top for nine years in a row? It is not because everyone is rich. It is because people trust each other and trust their government, and they know they will be looked after if something goes wrong. Healthcare and education are easy to access, and corruption is low.

India can learn from this, and some places have already started. In 2018, Delhi government schools started a Happiness Curriculum, where students spend time every day on mindfulness and talking about their feelings. Madhya Pradesh set up a state Department of Happiness. In 2022, India launched Tele-MANAS, a free mental health helpline. Our neighbour Bhutan even measures Gross National Happiness instead of only GDP.

Other steps India could take are: stronger community support, more counsellors in schools, cleaner and fairer government, and teaching people, especially teenagers, to use social media in a healthy way. The 2026 report found that using social media for more than about an hour a day is linked to lower well-being, and the global average is now around two and a half hours.

3. The problems and hurdles

But this is not easy. India has over 1.4 billion people, so any change has to reach a huge number of people in very different situations. There is still a big gap between rich and poor, and between cities and villages.

Mental health still has a stigma. Many people feel embarrassed to say they are struggling, so they do not ask for help. Students face a lot of pressure from exams and competition. Many adults work very long hours. And happiness is hard to measure, because it means different things in different cultures. Some people also argue that a poorer country should focus on growing its economy first.

Conclusion

So, where are we now? India has moved up from 144th in 2020 to 116th in 2026. That shows change is possible. But we still have a long way to go.

Change does not only come from the government. It can start with us: checking on a friend, putting the phone down for a while, and talking openly about how we feel.

Remember the number you thought of at the start. My hope is that one day, the average answer in India will be a lot higher than 4.54.

Thank you.

Criterion D: Thinking Critically

1. Stakeholders

These are the groups who are affected by happiness in India, or who can do something to improve it.

Government of India

Role: makes policies on health, education, jobs and corruption.

"Economic growth is our main goal, because it creates jobs and pulls people out of poverty. Programmes like Tele-MANAS show we also care about mental health."

Schools and teachers

Role: where young people spend most of their day.

"Happy students learn better. We want to add well-being lessons and counsellors, but exam results and the syllabus take up most of our time."

Students and teenagers

Role: the future of the country and heavy social media users.

"We feel a lot of pressure from exams and comparing ourselves online. We need more people to talk to and less judgement."

Parents and families

Role: the main source of social support in India.

"We want our children to be happy, but we also worry about their future and want them to do well in exams and get good jobs."

Employers and companies

Role: decide working hours and conditions for adults.

"Happy workers are more productive, but we also have targets to meet and need people to work hard."

NGOs and mental health organisations

Role: support people who are struggling and raise awareness.

"The biggest problem is stigma. People need to feel safe asking for help, and services need to reach villages, not just cities."

2. Perspectives

People disagree about whether India should focus on happiness or on the economy first.

Perspective 1: Economy first

  • Many Indians still need basic things like jobs, housing and healthcare.
  • GDP per capita is one of the 6 factors, so a richer India will become happier over time.
  • Happiness is hard to measure and means different things in different cultures.
  • The Cantril ladder is one survey question, so it may not show the full picture of India.

Perspective 2: Well-being first

  • India's economy has grown a lot, but its happiness rank is still low, so money is not enough.
  • Social support and trust can be improved without being a rich country.
  • Unhappy and stressed people are less healthy and less productive, which hurts the economy too.
  • Places like Delhi's Happiness Curriculum show that small steps are possible.

My view

I think both perspectives are right in some ways. India does need economic growth, but the data shows that growth alone has not made people much happier. I think India should grow its economy and at the same time invest in social support, mental health and healthy social media use, especially for young people.

3. The Situation in India

India is ranked 116th out of 147 countries in the 2026 World Happiness Report, with a life evaluation score of 4.536 out of 10 (about 4.54). This means that, on average, people in India rated their lives lower than people in many other countries. India is ranked below Nepal (99th) and Pakistan (104th).

However, the situation is not completely negative. India's position has improved from 144th in 2020 to 116th in 2026. This suggests progress, but the country still has challenges to address. These include improving social support, access to healthcare, freedom to make choices and trust in institutions.

The report uses people's answers about their lives over 2023–2025. The six factors help explain differences in happiness; they are not simply added together to create the ranking. Also, a country's rank does not explain every person's experience. The report gives India a rank confidence range of 109th–127th, so the exact position should not be treated as perfectly precise.

Why this matters: India needs to consider people's quality of life alongside economic growth. Better support systems, healthcare, education and trust can help improve well-being.

4. Why My Sources Are Reliable and Strong

I used a mixture of official reports, organisations and news articles. The strongest sources are the ones that provide original data or explain how the data was collected. News articles are useful for understanding the findings, but I use them alongside the original report rather than as my only evidence.

World Happiness Report

Reliability: Very strong

This is my main source because it publishes the actual international happiness rankings and explains its research method. It uses Gallup World Poll responses and provides statistical information, including confidence ranges. This makes it the best source for India's score and rank.

Gallup World Poll

Reliability: Very strong

Gallup collects the survey responses used for the report. It asks people to rate their lives using the same 0–10 Cantril Ladder question. Using a consistent question across countries makes the results easier to compare.

World Bank

Reliability: Very strong

The World Bank provides recognised economic and development statistics, such as GDP per capita and life expectancy. Its data methods and definitions are published, which helps researchers check where the information comes from.

Drishti IAS

Reliability: Strong for explanation

Drishti IAS is an educational and current-affairs organisation. Its article summarises the 2026 report in a way that is easier to understand. It is useful for background, but I check important statistics against the original World Happiness Report.

Utkarsh

Reliability: Useful secondary source

Utkarsh explains the main findings of the 2026 ranking in a student-friendly current-affairs format. It helps me understand the topic, but the original report is stronger evidence for exact figures.

Ecofin Agency

Reliability: Useful news source

Ecofin Agency reports on economic and development news, especially in Africa. It helps me understand regional comparisons, but I would check country rankings with the official report before using them as final evidence.

Governance Now

Reliability: Useful for historical context

This publication discusses governance and public policy. Its 2021 article can help explain earlier discussion about India's happiness ranking, but it is older and should not be used for 2026 statistics.

Telangana Today

Reliability: Useful for news coverage

Telangana Today is a regional news publication. Its coverage helps show how India's 2026 ranking was reported in the news. I use it to understand the public discussion, while checking the actual score and rank with the official report.

Overall Source Evaluation

My sources are strong because I do not depend on only one website. I compare the original World Happiness Report with Gallup's survey information and World Bank statistics. I also use news and educational sources to understand the topic from different viewpoints. However, I recognise that news articles are secondary sources and that the happiness ranking is based on survey answers, so it cannot show every person's experience.
